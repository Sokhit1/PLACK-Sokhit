## Worked example: building the cost of one trip

180-seat jet · 900 km · 1.5 block hours · 150 passengers (illustrative rates)

| Cost element | Driver × rate | Cost |
|---|---|---:|
| Fuel | 1.5 h × $2,400 | $3,600 |
| Crew | 1.5 h × $600 | $900 |
| Maintenance | 1.5 h × $500 | $750 |
| Aircraft ownership | 1.5 h × $800 | $1,200 |
| Airport and navigation | per flight | $1,200 |
| Passenger handling and fees | 150 × $8 | $1,200 |
| **Trip cost** | | **$8,850** |

Cost per passenger = 8,850 ÷ 150 = **$59** · CASK = 8,850 ÷ (180 × 900) ≈ **5.5 US cents**

## Worked example: break-even

Trip cost $14,000 · revenue $110 per passenger → 14,000 ÷ 110 = 127.3 → **128 passengers** → 128 ÷ 180 = **71% break-even load factor**

## Classroom exercise: evaluate two routes

*Group work, about 30 minutes. All numbers are invented for teaching.*

| Per flight | Route P | Route Q |
|---|---:|---:|
| Seats | 180 | 180 |
| Distance | 1,200 km | 600 km |
| Flights per day | 2 | 4 |
| Average passengers | 150 | 126 |
| Average fare | $130 | $85 |
| Ancillary per passenger | $15 | $10 |
| Cost per flight | $17,000 | $11,000 |

### Tasks

1. Load factor and revenue per flight.
2. Profit per flight and per day.
3. CASK, RASK and break-even load factor.
4. Recommend a strategy for each route.

Hint: revenue per passenger = fare + ancillary; ASK per flight = seats × distance.

---

## Solution

| Result | Route P | Route Q |
|---|---:|---:|
| Load factor | 150 ÷ 180 = 83.3% | 126 ÷ 180 = 70.0% |
| Revenue per flight | 150 × $145 = $21,750 | 126 × $95 = $11,970 |
| Profit per flight | $4,750 | $970 |
| **Profit per day** | **$9,500** | **$3,880** |
| CASK (US cents) | 17,000 ÷ 216,000 = 7.9 | 11,000 ÷ 108,000 = 10.2 |
| RASK (US cents) | 21,750 ÷ 216,000 = 10.1 | 11,970 ÷ 108,000 = 11.1 |
| Break-even load factor | 118 pax = 65.6% | 116 pax = 64.4% |

**Route P — grow.** Strong margin; consider a third daily flight if demand allows.

**Route Q — three flights, not four.** It carries 504 passengers a day. On three flights that is 168 per flight (93% load factor): 168 × $95 − $11,000 = $4,960 per flight, or **$14,880 a day** instead of $3,880, if passengers stay. One aircraft rotation is freed for another route. Risks: losing time-sensitive passengers who value four departures, and full flights at peak times.

## Review quiz

1. 1,710 of 2,000 flights arrive within 15 minutes. What is the on-time performance?
   A. 85.5% · B. 71% · C. 117%
2. A lease costs $9,000 a day. At 10 block hours a day, what is ownership cost per block hour?
   A. $90 · B. $900 · C. $9,000
3. Load factor is 80% and yield is 10 cents. What is PRASK?
   A. 8 cents · B. 12.5 cents · C. 18 cents
4. Trip cost is $12,000 and revenue is $100 per passenger. How many passengers to break even?
   A. 100 · B. 120 · C. 1,200
5. A route has negative contribution and low strategic value. What should the airline do?
   A. Grow it · B. Maintain it · C. Exit or suspend it

<details>
<summary><b>Answers</b></summary>

1. **A** — 1,710 ÷ 2,000 = 85.5%
2. **B** — 9,000 ÷ 10 = $900 per block hour
3. **A** — 0.80 × 10 = 8 cents
4. **B** — 12,000 ÷ 100 = 120 passengers
5. **C** — exit or suspend, and redeploy the aircraft

</details>
